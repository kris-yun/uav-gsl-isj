
Physics-Based Gas Mapping with Nano Aerial Vehicles:
The ADApprox Algorithm

Nicolaj Bösel-Schmid, Wanting Jin and Alcherio Martinoli

Abstract— Gas emissions play a crucial role in many envi-
ronmental and industrial processes, driving a growing effort to
understand their dispersion in air. Nonetheless, gas distribution
mapping is inherently challenging due to the complex interplay
between gas diffusion and wind flows. Mobile robots provide a
compelling alternative to static sensor networks for gas sensing,
having greater mobility and minimizing the need to permanently
deploy assets in the environment. However, robotic platforms
typically collect only sparse measurements due to constraints,
such as limited battery life, and state-of-the-art methods often
fail to accurately interpolate between scattered data. To address
this limitation, we introduce ADApprox, a novel gas mapping
algorithm. By leveraging the underlying physics which governs
gas dispersion, ADAapprox offers superior interpolation capa-
bilities. Our method locally approximates advection-diffusion
equation for an entire grid of points and learns the model
parameters from gas measurements. The learned parameters are
subsequently used to predict gas concentrations across the entire
environment. Extensive simulations and physical experiments
are conducted using a nano aerial vehicle. The mapping results
demonstrate that ADApprox consistently outperforms the state-
of-the-art algorithm Kernel DM+V/W while having a comparable
computational cost. In addition, we evaluate the effectiveness in
localizing a gas source based on the predicted gas maps. Our
findings indicate that ADApprox effectively localizes the gas
source, achieving a median error of 18 cm on an area of 12m2

in physical experiments.

I. INTRODUCTION

Understanding the dispersion of gases is essential for a
variety of applications. In industry, detecting and locating gas
leaks mitigates inadvertent emissions and helps to enforce
environmental regulations. Furthermore, there is an increasing
effort to monitor the air quality, especially in search and
rescue missions, where rapidly identifying harmful gases is
critical. All these scenarios directly relate to Gas Distribution
Mapping (GDM) and Gas Source Localization (GSL). GDM
focuses on characterizing the spatial distribution of gases in
an environment. Conversely, GSL aims to localize the gas
source as efficiently as possible. Both domains share common
challenges, including gas plume intermittency, turbulent
airflow, and the slow, noisy response of available low-cost
gas sensors.

Mobile robots are well-suited for GDM and GSL due to
their autonomous exploration capabilities and large coverage
compared to fixed sensor networks. A common solution is to

employ Nano Aerial Vehicles (NAVs), which cause reduced

while still capturing its three-dimensional nature. However,
NAVs are very energy-constrained, limiting them to spatially
sparse measurements. Additionally, gas sensing technology
that can be embedded on NAVs is constrained to in situ
measurements (i.e. measurements in the immediate proximity),
in contrast to many other sensors used in robotics, e.g. laser
range finders, that can infer information further away from
the robot. As a result, most state-of-the-art gas mapping
algorithms rely on local measurement extrapolation. Gas
dispersion is inherently governed by physical laws relating gas
concentration, airflow, and diffusion. Encoding this physical
knowledge into the model is expected to improve system
performance, especially when only scattered measurements
are available. Nevertheless, the underlying Partial Differ-
ential Equation (PDE) (advection-diffusion equation) has
no analytical solution. This has led to the development
of Computational Fluid Dynamic (CFD) techniques that
numerically approximate the physical equations. CFD-based
methods present two major limitations for robotic applications:
(1) High computational demands, making real-time execution
challenging. (2) The need for numerous physical parameters,
which are typically unknown and difficult to estimate.

To bridge the gap between purely statistical techniques
and CFD, we propose ADApprox — an Advection-Diffusion
Approximation-based GDM algorithm. ADApprox is a com-
putationally efficient method that balances a data-driven
approach with the inclusion of physical knowledge. Based
on scattered gas concentration measurements, the algorithm
reconstructs the gas concentration field and source release rate
on a discretized grid. The wind field is assumed to be steady
and known a priori. This study aims to enhance mapping
accuracy, improve sample efficiency, and increase model

II. RELATED WORK

This section discusses the most relevant GDM and GSL
works concerning our approach.

A. Gas Distribution Mapping

One of the earliest approaches in GDM is Kernel DM [2],
a relatively simple and computationally efficient method that
smooths and extrapolates measurements using a Gaussian
kernel. This algorithm has been extended to estimate variance
(Kernel DM+V) [3], consider wind flow (Kernel DM+V/W)
[4], and support 3D applications [1]. Other approaches
utilize Kalman filters [5] and Gaussian Mixture Regression
(GMR) [6] for GDM. The Kalman filter method follows a
Bayesian model. Compared to Kernel DM+V/W, it can be
seamlessly integrated into probabilistic frameworks. GMR
can predict new concentration values along with associated
uncertainties, making it particularly relevant for Informative
Path Planning (IPP). Although both works exploit sparse
variations of the algorithms and optimize the implementations,
they remain significantly slower than the Kernel DM variants.
Unlike previously discussed approaches, mapping based on
Gaussian Markov Random Field (GMRF) explicitly accounts
for obstacles in the environment [7]. GMRFs model the
gas distribution using factor graphs. Advection can be
incorporated as an additional energy function, linking the
gas concentration gradient to wind flow [8]. However, this
model is quite complex and requires the estimation of a lot of
hyperparameters (eight parameters for the energy functions,
weight of gas and wind measurements, and cell size). The
substantial computational burden of GMRFs can be alleviated
using message scheduling algorithms [9]. While Gaussian
functions offer mathematical convenience, only in simplified
cases can a Gaussian solution be deduced (e.g., Pseudo-
Gaussian plume model assumes constant, uniform airflow
with advection dominating over diffusion). Although some
of these works incorporate physical principles, none are
derived directly from the fundamental equations governing
gas dispersion. In contrast, our approach is derived from first
principles promising better inter- and extrapolation of gas
measurements and facilitating the model interpretability.

B. Gas Source Localization

GSL has been more extensively studied than GDM. Here,
we focus on GSL techniques that use GDM by extracting
analytical features, such as variance, maximal concentration,
or gas hit frequency. Prior work indicates that variance-
based localization outperforms maximum concentration-based
methods [1]. This is attributed to the intermittent nature of gas
plumes and the possibility of gas to accumulate away from the
source location. These findings are further supported in [10],
which uses a grid of Metal OXide (MOX) sensors to gather
gas measurements at different locations for a long period
of time. The authors compare variance-based GSL with the
‘bouts’ algorithm which identifies gas hits by detecting rising
edges in the first derivative of the gas concentration [11]. Their
results suggest that bout frequency provides more accurate
source localization than variance. However, bout analysis
relies on long measurement windows and requires manually
tuning the threshold amplitude to determine the bout. More
recently, GDM has also been integrated into probabilistic GSL
frameworks. For example, GaSLAM [12] builds gas maps
using the Kernel DM+V/W algorithm and estimates the source
position based on a gas distribution model (Pseudo-Gaussian
plume model). In [13], this simple model is replaced by a
more reliable gas distribution simulation (filament model [14])
to account for obstacles in the environment. Similar to some
CFD approaches, Wiedemann et al. suggest to numerically
approximate the PDE describing the diffusion of gas using
finite element method [15], [16]. The GSL technique is later
extended to incorporate wind flow [17]. Our work also derives
from the advection-diffusion equation, but unlike Wiedemann
et al., we propose to locally approximate the gas concentration
for an entire field of points by using a Taylor expansion.
This results in a more computationally efficient model with
significantly reduced complexity.

III. METHODOLOGY

Our algorithm has two major steps: in the first one, we
learn optimal parameters from data by locally approximating
the advection-diffusion equation for a grid of points spanning
the environment and formulating it as least square problem. In
the second step, the algorithm predicts concentration values
at new positions using the optimal parameters and estimates
the corresponding variances.

key processes: advection, which represents the transport of
the substance due to a velocity field, and diffusion, which
describes the spreading of the substance due to molecular
motion and concentration gradients. In this work, we focus
on gas dispersion in the air:

E. Time Complexity

Computing the inverse of (XTA2X +R) ∈ RMC×MC di-
rectly is computationally expensive. However, (XTA2X+R)
is a block diagonal matrix with M×M submatrices, allowing
optimization via the Schur complement [21]. This reduces the
complexity from O(M3C3) to O(M3C). Additionally, stor-
ing matrices composed by diagonal blocks as 3D arrays (e.g.,
X ∈ RN×M×C rather than X ∈ RNC×MC) significantly
improves memory efficiency. These optimizations yield a
final complexity of O(NM2C + M3C). Since M << N ,
the overall complexity simplifies to O(NM2C), which also
holds for the prediction step of N concentration values and
their variances.

IV. SIMULATION EXPERIMENTS

In this section, ADApprox is tested for both GDM and
GSL tasks in simulation. First, the experimental setup, the
benchmarking algorithm, and the evaluation metrics are
introduced.

A. Experimental Setup

1) Software: The simulations are conducted in Webots
[22], a high-fidelity open-source simulator. Gas dispersion is
modeled using a filament-based approach [14]. By default, the
wind velocity is set to 0.7m s−1 in the negative x-direction.
For the experiments analyzing the effect of wind speed, we
evaluate three different wind speeds: 0.3m s−1, 0.7m s−1,
and 1.3m s−1.

2) Trajectory: We employ a fixed sweeping motion, a
standard trajectory in gas sensing research [3], [6], [23], since
the path planning is not the primary focus of this work. The
scanning path consists of evenly spaced crosswind transitions
(see white to red path in Fig. 2 g). By default, the spacing
between each transition is 1m. For the experiments analyzing
the effect of trajectory spacing, we evaluate three different
spacing values: 1m, 1.5m, and 2m. The drone maintains
a constant velocity of 0.1m s−1 throughout all experiments.
Due to battery limitations in the physical experiments, the
Crazyflie drone completes only a single horizontal scan at
the source height (z = 0.75m). As a result, the algorithm is
tested solely in a 2D setting.

B. Benchmark Algorithm

The Kernel DM+V/W map [1] is selected as the state-of-
the-art benchmark algorithm. This method is widely used in
GDM due to its demonstrated effectiveness in both indoor
[1], [12], [23] and outdoor [4] environments, and its relatively
straightforward implementation. Similar to our approach,
it estimates variance and incorporates wind information to
enhance mapping accuracy. Kernel DM+V/W has three key
hyperparameters: kernel width σ0, influence radius rinf , and
kernel stretching factor γ. The optimal hyperparameters are
determined via exhaustive grid search and optimizing the
plume shape metric (see Section IV-C), yielding σ0 = 0.05,
rinf = 1.0, and γ = 0.7 for simulations, and σ0 = 0.04,
rinf = 0.8, and γ = 0.8 for physical experiments. The
confidence scaling parameter σΩ is set as N (0, σ0), as
recommended in [3], [4]. Kernel DM+V/W normalizes the
measurements within the range [0, 1]. To compute the Mean
Absolute Error (MAE), concentration values are subsequently
rescaled using the maximum mean measurement per cell
(maximum of Fig. 2 g).

D. Gas Distribution Mapping Performance

Fig. 3 presents the simulation results. The proposed
algorithm is evaluated using three different learning-grid
resolutions: 0.1m, 0.2m, and 0.4m. The resolution indicates
the size of the grid cells during parameter learning and
determines the total number of cells C. Gas map predictions
of ADApprox and Kernel DM+V/W are always computed at
the resolution of 0.1m for direct comparison with the GT.
In terms of shape coverage, the proposed algorithm outper-
forms Kernel DM+V/W across all learning-grid resolutions.
For the MAE, the resolutions of 0.1m and 0.2m exhibit
improvements while a very coarse resolution of 0.4m does
not. The computational time is defined including learning
the parameters and predicting the concentration and variance
map. As expected, it scales approximately quadratically with
the grid size, given the algorithm’s linear dependency on the
number of cells C. In Tables I and II, we also evaluate the
system robustness under varying wind speeds and scanning
trajectory spacings, with a learning-grid resolution of 0.1m.
The performance of both methods improve with stronger
winds and denser measurement sampling. However, Kernel
DM+V/W performs significantly worse when the spacing
exceeds 1.5m.

In all tested configurations, ADApprox consistently out-
performs the benchmark method. Only for a learning grid
resolution that is four times lower (0.4m), Kernel DM+V/W
has a better MAE than our method, while for the same
resolution the MAE of ADApprox is about three times
smaller. In particular, ADApprox shows a superior ability
to interpolate and extrapolate measurements, which can be
seen by looking at the results of different sweeping motion
spacings. ADApprox accurately recovers the shape of the
gas plume even for a spacing of 2m that results in only
three crossings in front of the gas source. Meanwhile, the
shape metric of Kernel DM+V/W worsens significantly for
the same measurements due to its limited influence radius.
This shows the capability of ADApprox to handle sparse data,
which is crucial when using robotic systems, in particular
NAVs. Although the computational time of both methods
scales linearly with N and C, the Kernel DM+V/W algorithm
reduces the complexity by considering only a local subset of
cells for each measurement (influence radius in [1]). Notably,

conditions that enhance the reproducibility of experiments.
The NAV is localized using a Motion Capture System
(MCS) 3 with millimetric accuracy. An ethanol gas source is
used due to its non-toxic properties and ease of handling. The
GT is obtained by mounting the Crazyflie on a traversing
system, a 3D linear robot that allows precise movement
throughout the wind tunnel.

2) Sensor: The Crazyflie is equipped with a custom printed
circuit board designed in [23], which integrates the MOX gas
sensor MiCS-5524 4. The sensor is mounted at the bottom
of the Crazyflie, as this configuration demonstrated superior
performance compared to a top-mounted placement [25]. The
sensor operates at a sampling frequency of 10Hz, with raw
measurements recorded in millivolts.

3) Data Filtering: Raw gas measurements are inherently
noisy and require filtering. First, the offset of non-gas readings
is determined separately for each experiment to remove the
background noise of the sensor. Next, high-frequency noise
is attenuated by applying a moving average filter with a 1 s
window. Due to the chemical properties of MOX sensors,
measurements are delayed and distorted with respect to the
true concentration value. Therefore, the slow sensor dynamics
are modeled by a 2 s delay and a first-order system with a
time constant of 0.6 s.

VI. CONCLUSION

This work proposes a novel Gas Distribution Mapping
(GDM) algorithm: ADApprox - an Advection-Diffusion
Approximation. Unlike many previous methods, ADApprox
is rooted strongly in the underlying physics by locally approx-
imating the advection-diffusion equation and formulating a
computationally efficient least-squares solution. The algorithm
is benchmarked against the Kernel DM+V/W mapping algo-
rithm [1] through both simulation and physical experiments.
ADApprox consistently outperforms the benchmark method
in both accuracy and shape coverage of the estimated gas
map. Our method has a computational complexity similar to
that of Kernel DM+V/W and can be speeded up by lowering
the grid resolution where the approximation parameters are
learned. In addition, ADApprox exhibits greater robustness
to sparsity of available gas measurements.

Furthermore, the estimated gas map’s effectiveness for Gas
Source Localization (GSL) is evaluated using the maximum
concentration, variance, and release rate parameter as source
predictor. The release rate parameter ω

(3)
c of ADApprox

correlates with the release rate Rc. It demonstrates superior
accuracy in GSL, achieving a median localization error
of 18 cm in physical experiments. These findings highlight
the potential of grounding mapping algorithms in physical
equations for improving both the accuracy and interpretability
of the estimated gas map, one of the primary objectives of
this work.

Future research should test ADApprox in 3D environments
and more complex scenarios involving obstacles. This will
require estimating a wind flow that is no longer uniform.
Finally, we aim to enhance GSL efficiency through a dedicated
path-planning module including a gas source identification
criterion.
